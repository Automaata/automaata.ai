Duty-free rest breaks in California: what Augustus v. ABM means for a guard schedule

The 2016 California Supreme Court ruling that cost one security company a $90 million judgment, the rule it set, and how to schedule and document rest breaks so your operation can show compliance.

In December 2016 the California Supreme Court decided Augustus v. ABM Security Services. The facts were ordinary: ABM's guards were told to keep their radios and pagers on during rest breaks and to respond if something came up. No guard in the record was shown to have had a break actually interrupted. The court held that this policy alone violated California law, and reinstated a judgment of roughly $90 million for a class of about 14,000 guards. The case had been running since 2005.

The rule

California's Wage Order 4 requires a paid, ten-minute rest period for every four hours worked or major fraction of it; in practice, one break for shifts of 3.5 to 6 hours, two for 6 to 10 hours, three for 10 to 14. The court's holding in Augustus is that during a rest period the employer must relieve the employee of all duties and give up control over how the employee spends the time. An "on-call" rest break, where the guard must stay ready to respond, is not a rest break. In the court's words, a rest period must be a period of rest.

The consequence for a guard company is that the usual workaround, "take your ten but keep the radio on", is exactly the policy that was found unlawful. A guard on break is not on post, and someone else has to be.

What it means for the schedule

Compliance is a scheduling problem before it is a policy problem. For a single-officer post, a duty-free break means either a relief officer, a supervisor who covers, or a client who has agreed that the post is unstaffed for ten minutes at a known time. For multi-officer sites it means staggering breaks so coverage never drops below the contracted level. Either way the break has to exist in the schedule as a block of time with a cover plan, not as an instruction to "find a moment".

The court did note a narrow path: an employer can seek an exemption from the Division of Labor Standards Enforcement for on-duty rest periods where the nature of the work makes duty-free breaks impossible. That is an application, not a policy a company can adopt on its own.

What it means for the record

When a rest-break claim is made, the question is whether the company can show that breaks were authorised, permitted and duty-free. Three records matter:

  1. The schedule, showing break windows and who covered the post.
  2. The officer's attestation at the end of the shift that breaks were taken and were duty-free, or a note that one was missed and why. A missed break owes one hour of premium pay under Labor Code 226.7, and a company that pays it when due is in a far better position than one that discovers the pattern in discovery.
  3. The activity record. A checkpoint scan, a report filed or a dispatch acknowledged during a logged break is evidence that the break was not duty-free. Seeing those exceptions weekly is how a company fixes a site before it becomes a class.
How Automaata does it. Break rules are an entity in the platform, not a policy memo. The scheduler treats duty-free breaks as a hard constraint and will not propose a roster without cover for them. Officers attest to breaks at clock-out. Any scan, report or dispatch during a logged break is flagged as an exception for the supervisor, and missed-break premiums are computed into the pay run for a person to approve.

Checklist

  • Write the rest-break policy so it says, plainly, that officers are relieved of all duties and need not carry a radio or phone on break.
  • Put breaks in the schedule with a cover plan for every single-officer post.
  • Collect a break attestation every shift; pay the premium when a break is missed.
  • Review break-time activity exceptions weekly by site.
  • Confirm the current rules with employment counsel; wage orders and case law change, and this guide is general information rather than legal advice.

Sources: Augustus v. ABM Security Services, Inc., 2 Cal.5th 257 (2016); analyses by Cooley, Goodwin, Ogletree Deakins, Seyfarth Shaw and Fisher Phillips; California Industrial Welfare Commission Wage Order 4.
